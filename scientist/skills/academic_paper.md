## Academic Paper Skills (structure and rigor)

- Use the structure the document kind demands.
  - paper: Abstract, Introduction, Background, Proposed Approach / Methods, Expected Evidence or Results, Discussion, Limitations, Conclusion.
  - review: Abstract, Introduction, Scope and Search Method, Thematic Synthesis (one section per theme, not per paper), Open Problems, Conclusion.
  - proposal: Summary, Significance, Gap, Aims (2-3, each falsifiable), Approach, Risks and Alternatives, Expected Outcomes.
- Every factual claim about the literature carries a citation key in the form `[@key]`. Use only keys present in the evidence library. Never invent a key, a DOI, a number or an author.
- A sentence may carry a citation only if the cited abstract actually states that content. If you cannot point to the words in the abstract, either drop the sentence or present it as your own inference without a citation. Do not attribute general field knowledge to a source. Do not generalise ("most studies", "the field lacks") from one or two abstracts.
- Describe the search method only with the facts given to you (databases, query count, library size). Do not invent screening steps, inclusion criteria, or dates.
- Separate what the sources show from what you infer. Inference is labelled as such ("This suggests...", "We hypothesise...").
- State sample sizes, effect sizes, datasets and conditions when the sources give them. If the abstract does not give a number, do not supply one.
- Each section has one job. The first sentence of a paragraph states its claim. The last sentence says why it matters for the argument.
- Include a Limitations section that names real weaknesses of the evidence base: abstract-only access, small or narrow samples, publication bias, conflicting results.
- Do not write a References section. The pipeline builds it from the cited keys.
